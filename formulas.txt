==================================================================================
[1] EXACT AREA OF THE KERR SHADOW FOR AN EDGE-ON OBSERVER (ANY SPIN)
==================================================================================
Question (plain English):
  A black hole photographed against a bright background looks like a dark patch, the
  "shadow" (this is what the Event Horizon Telescope images of M87* and Sgr A* show).
  For a non-spinning black hole the shadow is a disc of area 27*pi*M^2. Spin squashes and
  shifts it. People use the shadow AREA (or the "equivalent diameter") to measure black holes,
  but for a spinning hole it has only been computed numerically, by fits, or to first order
  in the spin. Is there an exact formula?  Yes, at least for an observer in the equatorial
  plane (seeing the hole edge-on).

Formula (M = 1, spin 0 < a < 1, observer at infinity, inclination 90 deg):
  Let r_p < r_r be the prograde and retrograde circular photon-orbit radii,
      r_p = 2[1 + cos((2/3) arccos(-a))],  r_r = 2[1 + cos((2/3) arccos(a))],
  and r_0 = 6 - r_p - r_r   (the third root of r^3 - 6r^2 + 9r - 4a^2; 0 < r_0 < 1).

  (a) One-line integral form:
        A(a) = 3 * Integral_{r_p}^{r_r}  [ (r-1)(5r-2) + 2(1-a^2)/(r-1) ] dr
                                         / sqrt( r (4a^2 - r (r-3)^2) )
      The square root is sqrt( r (r-r_0)(r-r_p)(r_r-r) ).

  (b) Closed form in complete elliptic integrals K, E, Pi (parameter convention m = k^2,
      Pi(n|m) = Int_0^{K} du / (1 - n sn^2 u)):
        m  = (r_r-r_p) r_0 / ((r_r-r_0) r_p),    n = (r_r-r_p)/(r_r-r_0),
        n' = n (r_0-1)/(r_p-1),                  g = 2/sqrt((r_r-r_0) r_p),
        V  = [ n E + (m-n) K + (2nm + 2n - n^2 - 3m) Pi(n) ] / ( 2 (n-1)(m-n) )
        I0 = g K
        I1 = g [ r_0 K + (r_p-r_0) Pi(n) ]
        I2 = g [ r_0^2 K + 2 r_0 (r_p-r_0) Pi(n) + (r_p-r_0)^2 V ]
        J  = g [ K/(r_0-1) + (r_0-r_p)/((r_p-1)(r_0-1)) Pi(n') ]
        A(a) = 6 I0 - 21 I1 + 15 I2 + 6 (1-a^2) J          (times M^2)
      (all K, E, Pi evaluated at parameter m).

  (c) Small-spin series (coefficients found to 40+ digits and recognised as exact rationals):
        A(a) / (27 pi M^2) = 1 - a^2/18 - a^4/72 - 2059 a^6/314928
                               - 88175 a^8/22674816 - 355999 a^10/136048896 - ...
      (314928 = 2^4 3^9; 22674816 = 2^7 3^11; 136048896 = 2^9 3^11 ... all denominators 2^p 3^q)

  (d) Near-extremal expansion (NEW in this entry; epsilon = 1 - a, M = 1):
        A = 16 pi + 15 sqrt(3) + (3 pi/sqrt 2) epsilon^(1/2)
              + epsilon [ (8/sqrt 3) ln(27/(2 epsilon)) - 6 sqrt(3) ] + o(epsilon)
      i.e. the area rises steeply (like a square root) as the spin drops below maximal.
      How found: closed form (b) at 60-150 digits for epsilon = 1e-12 ... 1e-80.
        (A - A_1)/sqrt(eps) = 6.66432440723787 (eps=1e-30), 6.664324407237549374794 (1e-40),
        6.66432440723754937052387 (1e-50);  3 pi/sqrt2 = 6.66432440723754937047...
        (differences 3.2e-13, 4.3e-18, 5.3e-23: shrink like sqrt(eps), as expected).
        Next: [A - A_1 - (3pi/sqrt2) sqrt(eps)]/eps - (8/sqrt3) ln(1/eps) = 1.629003878653755218921686
        at both eps = 1e-60 and 1e-80; PSLQ relation 3b + 18 sqrt3 + 8 sqrt3 ln2 - 24 sqrt3 ln3 = 0,
        i.e. b = (8/sqrt3) ln(27/2) - 6 sqrt3. (The ln coefficient 8/sqrt3 = 4.6188 was read off
        from the growth per decade, 10.635/ln 10.)
      Derivation of the sqrt(eps) term (matched asymptotics): as a -> 1 the roots r_0, r_p merge
      at r = 1, r_{p,0} = 1 +- delta, delta = sqrt(8 eps/3). In the inner region r - 1 = delta x
      the pole term 6(1-a^2)/(r-1) ~ 12 eps/(r-1) dominates, sqrt(W) ~ sqrt3 delta sqrt(x^2-1), and
      Int_1^inf dx/(x sqrt(x^2-1)) = pi/2, giving (12 eps/(sqrt3 delta)) (pi/2) = (3 pi/sqrt2) sqrt(eps).
      Status of (d): sqrt(eps) term derived (leading-order asymptotics); the eps ln eps and eps
      terms identified numerically only.

Decimal values (M = 1):
  a = 0.1   : A = 84.77575938950222861469736...
  a = 0.5   : A = 83.56105412846154557835349...
  a = 0.9   : A = 79.58712552387277213416654...
  a = 0.998 : A = 76.60485539230466491847881...
  a -> 1    : A = 16 pi + 15 sqrt(3) = 76.24624457096985121831...   (entry [2])
  a = 0     : A = 27 pi = 84.82300164692441743849...

How it was derived:
  The shadow edge is the curve (alpha, beta) = (-xi, +-sqrt(eta)) traced by the spherical
  photon orbits, with xi = (r^2(3-r) - a^2(r+1))/(a(r-1)),
  eta = r^3(4a^2 - r(r-3)^2)/(a^2 (r-1)^2).  Area = 2 Int sqrt(eta) |dxi/dr| dr.
  Key simplification: dxi/dr = -2[(r-1)^3 + 1 - a^2]/(a (r-1)^2), so the integrand is
  (4/a^2) r sqrt(W) [1 + (1-a^2)/(r-1)^3] with W = r(4a^2 - r(r-3)^2).
  A computer-algebra reduction (sympy; code/reduce_area.py) subtracts an exact derivative
  d/dr[S(r) sqrt(W)] (which vanishes at both ends) and leaves the integrand
  (6 - 21 r + 15 r^2 + 6(1-a^2)/(r-1)) / sqrt(W) -- surprisingly, the polynomial part
  does not depend on a at all.  The standard substitution
  sn^2 u = (r_r-r_0)(r-r_p)/((r_r-r_p)(r-r_0)) then gives the K, E, Pi form.
  The small-spin coefficients were obtained by fitting a 30-term polynomial in a^2 to the
  exact formula evaluated at 250-digit precision, and recognising each coefficient as a
  rational with denominator 2^p 3^q (agreement to > 25 digits).

How it was verified (code/check_reduce.py, code/area_closed.py, code/verify_shoelace.py):
  * Original curve integral vs closed form, 30-digit arithmetic:
      a=0.5: 83.5610541284615455783534950008 (direct) vs 83.5610541284615455783534950007 (closed)
      a=0.9: 79.5871255238727721341665423326 (direct) vs 79.5871255238727721341665423326 (closed)
  * Reduced integral (a) by numerical quadrature vs closed form (b), 40 digits: agree to ~1e-19
    at a = 0.001, 0.3, 0.7, 0.9, 0.998, 0.9999999.
  * Fully independent check: shoelace (polygon) area of 800,000 points on the shadow edge:
      a=0.2: 84.63258477 (polygon) vs 84.63258477162 (formula)
      a=0.8: 81.07754800 (polygon) vs 81.07754800229
      a=0.999: 76.49048639 (polygon) vs 76.49048638587
  * Limits: a -> 0 gives 27 pi; a -> 1 gives 16 pi + 15 sqrt(3) (entry [2]).
  * The a^2 term, -3 pi a^2/2, agrees with the perturbative result of Hassanabadi et al.,
    arXiv:2607.11979 (2026), eq. (115): A = 27 pi m^2 - (3 pi/2) a^2 + O(a^4).

Novelty status: NOT FOUND (exact formula, and the series beyond a^2).
  The a^2 correction is KNOWN (arXiv:2607.11979). A January 2026 paper (arXiv:2601.09655,
  "closed-form surrogate for the equivalent diameter of the Kerr shadow") fits the area with
  a 15-parameter polynomial, and says it only has an exact result for the face-on (polar)
  case, which suggests the exact edge-on formula is not standard. Not found in web searches.

[2] AREA OF THE EDGE-ON SHADOW OF AN EXTREMAL KERR BLACK HOLE
==================================================================================
Question (plain English):
  A black hole spinning as fast as nature allows (a = M) has a shadow with one flattened,
  straight edge when seen from its equator. How big is it?

Formula:
  A_extremal(edge-on) = (16 pi + 15 sqrt(3)) M^2  = 76.24624457096985121831398925506... M^2
  i.e. 89.888642338...% of the non-spinning value 27 pi M^2.

How it was derived:
  For a = 1, xi = 1 + 2r - r^2 and eta = r^3 (4 - r), 1 <= r <= 4, so
  A = 4 Int_1^4 r (r-1) sqrt(r(4-r)) dr, which is elementary (sympy evaluates it exactly).
  (The straight "NHEK line" segment of the edge sits at constant alpha = -2 and adds
  nothing to Int beta d(alpha).)

How it was verified:
  * sympy exact integral -> 15 sqrt(3) + 16 pi.
  * mpmath quadrature (40 digits): 76.2462445709698512183139892550601316513;
    PSLQ on {A, pi, sqrt(3)} returns the relation A - 16 pi - 15 sqrt(3) = 0.
  * Limit of the general formula [1]: a = 0.9999999 gives 76.248359...,
    a = 0.999999 gives 76.25297..., approaching 76.24624 (the approach is like sqrt(1-a)).

Novelty status: KNOWN. Found after recording: P. Cunha, C. Herdeiro, E. Radu,
  "EHT constraint on the ultralight scalar hair of the M87 supermassive black hole",
  Universe 5 (2019) 220, arXiv:1909.08039, which gives A = (15 sqrt(3) + 16 pi) M^2.
  Kept here only as the a -> 1 check of entry [1]. That paper (and arXiv:2209.06237, which
  follows it) states that the shadow area can be computed exactly only in two cases
  (observer on the axis; extremal spin seen edge-on) and interpolates in between --
  this supports the novelty of entries [1] and [3].

[3] SHADOW AREA OF AN EXTREMAL KERR BLACK HOLE SEEN FROM ANY ANGLE
==================================================================================
Question (plain English):
  For a black hole spinning at the maximum rate (a = M), how big is its shadow when we view
  it from an arbitrary angle theta (theta = 0: looking down the spin axis; theta = 90 deg:
  side-on)?  Until now exact answers were known only at theta = 0 (a circle of area
  pi(12 + 8 sqrt 2) M^2) and theta = 90 deg (entry [2]); everything else was numerical
  or interpolated.

Key new fact: with s = sin(theta), c = cos(theta), the polynomial that defines the shadow
  edge factorises into two quadratics:
      s^2 [ r^3(4-r) + c^2 - (c^2/s^2)(r^2-2r-1)^2 ]
          = -( r^2 - 2(1+s) r - c^2 ) ( r^2 - 2(1-s) r - c^2 )
  so its four roots are elementary:
      r_A = 1-s-sqrt(2-2s) < r_B = 1+s-sqrt(2+2s) < r_C = 1-s+sqrt(2-2s) < r_D = 1+s+sqrt(2+2s).

Formula (M = 1, observer at infinity):
  Let R(r) = (r-r_A)(r-r_B)(r-r_C)(r_D-r). Then
     A(theta) = 4 * Integral_{r_lo}^{r_D} (3 r^2 - cos^2 theta) / sqrt(R(r)) dr  +  B(theta)
  where
   * if theta <= theta_c:  r_lo = r_C  and B = 0;
   * if theta >  theta_c:  r_lo = 1    and B = (2 + s^2) sqrt(3 s^2 + s^2 c^2 - 4 c^2) / s^2
     (this is the case where the shadow edge has its straight "NHEK line");
   * theta_c = arcsin(sqrt(3) - 1) = 47.0585971351200897... deg  (where r_C = 1).
  Closed form: with a=r_D, b=r_C, cc=r_B, d=r_A,
     m = (a-b)(cc-d)/((a-cc)(b-d)),  n = (a-b)/(a-cc),  g = 2/sqrt((a-cc)(b-d)),
     I0 = g K(m),  I2 = g [ cc^2 K + 2 cc (b-cc) Pi(n|m) + (b-cc)^2 V ],
     V = [ n E + (m-n) K + (2nm+2n-n^2-3m) Pi(n|m) ] / (2(n-1)(m-n)),
     A(theta) = 4 (3 I2 - cos^2(theta) I0)      for theta <= theta_c,
  and for theta > theta_c the same expression with incomplete integrals over the
  amplitude range [phi_0, pi/2], sin^2 phi_0 = (a-cc)(1-b)/((a-b)(1-cc)), plus B(theta)
  (code/ext_closed.py implements both).

  Near the axis (series in s = sin theta, coefficients identified exactly by PSLQ):
     A = pi (12 + 8 sqrt 2) + sqrt(2) pi [ s^2/2 + s^4/128 - 7 s^6/2048 - 1735 s^8/524288 - ... ]
  (only even powers; all denominators are powers of 2).
  Near-extremal behaviour (spin a slightly below M, epsilon = 1 - a):
     A(a, theta) = A_ext(theta) + c(theta) sqrt(epsilon) + ...,
     c(theta) = pi (3 - 6 cos^2 theta - cos^4 theta) / (sqrt(2) sin^3 theta)   for theta > theta_c,
     c(theta) = 0 (correction only O(epsilon))                                   for theta < theta_c.
  Since the straight NHEK-line edge has half-length beta_m with
  beta_m^2 = (3 - 6c^2 - c^4)/s^2, this is c = pi beta_m^2/(sqrt2 sin theta): the square-root
  growth comes entirely from the NHEK line opening up. Edge-on: c = 3 pi/sqrt2 (entry [1](d)).
  Check (code/nearext_incl.py, nearext_incl2.py; master formula [4] at 50 digits, eps = 1e-10
  and 1e-12, crude extrapolation):
     50 deg: 1.7307038 vs predicted 1.7307190;  60 deg: 4.9164299 vs 4.9164410;
     75 deg: ~6.39299 vs 6.3930;  85 deg: 6.6384383 vs 6.6384460;
     30 deg (< theta_c): (A - A_ext)/sqrt(eps) = 3.9e-3, 3.9e-4, 3.9e-5 at eps = 1e-8, 1e-10, 1e-12
     -> 0 as predicted.
  Derivation (same asymptotics as [1](d), applied to the master formula [4]): at a = 1 - eps,
  N(1) = -4(1-a^2)(1-u) ~ -8 eps sin^2(theta), and near r = 1, N ~ N_1 (r-1)^2 - 8 eps s^2 with
  N_1 = 3 - 6c^2 - c^4, so the two lowest roots sit at r = 1 +- delta, delta = s sqrt(8 eps/N_1).
  The pole coefficient of [4] is -2(a^2-1)(u^2+6u-3)/(u-1) ~ 4 eps N_1/s^2, and the inner
  integral gives (4 eps N_1/s^2) * pi/(2 delta sqrt(N_1)) = pi N_1 sqrt(eps)/(sqrt2 s^3) = c(theta).
  For theta < theta_c, N_1 < 0: no merging roots above r = 1, so no sqrt(eps) term.
  At theta = 90 deg it reproduces (16 pi + 15 sqrt 3); at theta = 30 deg the elliptic
  modulus is k = sin 15 deg, a "singular value", so K and E there reduce to Gamma(1/3).

Decimal values (M = 1):
  theta = 0          : 73.24217534834444883767876851984   (= pi(12+8 sqrt 2), known)
  theta = 17 deg (M87*): 73.4323101634318012561004938381
  theta = 30 deg     : 73.7993967474284232435635948378
  theta = 45 deg     : 74.3581830819766845526236405803
  theta = theta_c    : 74.4382108812718860430257575665
  theta = 60 deg     : 75.0887577305781309271336402814
  theta = 80 deg     : 76.0911861732286858492383919946
  theta = 90 deg     : 76.2462445709698512183139892551  (= 16 pi + 15 sqrt 3, known)

How it was derived:
  For a = 1: xi = 1 + 2r - r^2, eta = r^3(4-r); celestial coords alpha = -xi/s,
  beta^2 = eta + c^2 - xi^2 c^2/s^2, area = 2 Int beta |d alpha|
  = (4/s) Int (r-1) sqrt(beta^2) dr.  sympy factorised the quartic (code/extremal_incl.py
  printed nice roots at 30 deg: 3/2 and 3/2+sqrt3, which led to the general pattern
  p^2 = 4 - 4c^2 => p = 2 sin theta). Then sympy reduction (code/reduce_ext.py):
  x sqrt(P) = s^2 (3x^2 + 6x + s^2 + 2)/sqrt(P) + d/dx[ (x^2 - s^2 - 2) sqrt(P)/4 ], x = r-1,
  which after shifting back gives (3r^2 - cos^2 theta) and the boundary term B.

How it was verified (30-digit arithmetic):
  * Original area integral vs elliptic closed form, all 30 digits agree, e.g.
      17 deg: 73.4323101634318012561004938381 (both)
      60 deg: 75.0887577305781309271336402814 (both)
      80 deg: 76.0911861732286858492383919946 vs ...9947
  * Reduced one-line integral vs original at 0.5,10,30,45,47.06,50,60,80,90 deg: agree to 1e-25.
  * Independent polygon (shoelace) area of the shadow edge, 200,000 points:
      30 deg: 73.79939224 vs 73.79939675;  45 deg: 74.35818077 vs 74.35818082;
      75 deg: 75.90649993 vs 75.90650254  (agreement limited by polygon resolution).
  * Limits theta -> 0 and theta = 90 deg reproduce the two known exact values.
  * Series coefficients: fitted from 24 sample points at 200-digit precision; PSLQ relations
    2c_2 = sqrt2 pi, 128 c_4 = sqrt2 pi, 2048 c_6 = -7 sqrt2 pi, 524288 c_8 = -1735 sqrt2 pi
    found with tolerance 1e-20; the series reproduces A(17 deg) to 5 digits with 4 terms.

Novelty status: NOT FOUND. The edge-curve of the extremal Kerr shadow is studied
  (e.g. arXiv:2405.08875 gives exact escape cones), but no exact area for general
  inclination was found; papers using the area (arXiv:1909.08039, 2209.06237) say it is
  only exactly known on the axis and edge-on, and interpolate in between (error ~0.8%).
  The factorisation of the edge polynomial may well be known implicitly to experts.

[4] MASTER FORMULA: KERR SHADOW AREA FOR ANY SPIN AND ANY INCLINATION
==================================================================================
Question (plain English):
  Same as [1], but for an observer at ANY angle theta from the spin axis. For general spin
  and angle the shadow edge is described by a degree-6 polynomial, so the area cannot be
  written with ordinary (complete elliptic) functions -- but can it at least be written as
  ONE simple, fast, derivative-free integral?  Yes, and the integrand is very simple.

Formula (M = 1, 0 < a < 1, 0 < theta < pi, observer at infinity). Put u = a^2 cos^2(theta).
  N(r) = -r^6 + 6 r^5 - (9 + 2u) r^4 + 4 a^2 r^3 + 6u r^2 - 4 a^2 u r - u^2 (r-1)^2
  (N(r) = [beta^2] * a^2 (r-1)^2 sin^2(theta), the "height^2" of the shadow edge, cleared of
  denominators). Let r_1 < r_2 be the two roots of N with r > 1 (the radii of the spherical
  photon orbits that touch the edge). Then
     A(a,theta) = 1/(u-1) * Integral_{r_1}^{r_2} [ 2(u^2 - 5a^2 u + 6u + 3a^2 - 3)
                                       - 2(2u^2 + a^2 u - 3u - 3a^2 + 3) r
                                       + 2(u^2 - 6u - 3) r^2
                                       + 4(u + 3) r^3
                                       + 2(u - 3) r^4
                                       - 2(a^2 - 1)(u^2 + 6u - 3)/(r - 1) ]  dr / sqrt(N(r))
  At theta = 90 deg (u = 0) this reduces to entry [1]; the integrand is integrable at both
  ends (inverse-square-root endpoints), so tanh-sinh quadrature gets 15-30 digits instantly.

Decimal values (M = 1):

[6] ESCAPE PROBABILITY OF LIGHT FROM THE HORIZON OF AN EXTREMAL (KERR-NEWMAN) BLACK HOLE
==================================================================================
Question (plain English):
  Put a lamp just outside the horizon of a black hole spinning at the maximum rate. The lamp
  shines equally in all directions (in its own local, "non-rotating" frame -- the ZAMO frame).
  What fraction of its light escapes to the outside universe?  For an ordinary black hole the
  answer tends to 0 at the horizon, but for a maximally spinning one a finite fraction escapes.
  For an uncharged black hole with the lamp on the equator this is known to be exactly 7/24.
  We find the exact answer for every latitude and for charged (Kerr-Newman) black holes too.

Formula (extremal Kerr-Newman: a^2 + Q^2 = M^2, a > 0; lamp at polar angle theta, r -> M):
     k = (M^2 + a^2 cos^2 theta) / (2 a M sin theta)
     P_escape = 1/2 - arcsin(k)/(2 pi) - k/4      if k < 1,      P_escape = 0  if k >= 1.
  Special cases:
     * uncharged (a = M), equator:  k = 1/2,  P = 7/24 = 0.2916666...          (known)
     * charged, equator:            P = 1/2 - arcsin(M/2a)/(2 pi) - M/(8a),  nonzero iff a > M/2
     * uncharged, latitude theta:   k = (1 + cos^2 theta)/(2 sin theta), nonzero iff
                                    sin theta > sqrt(3) - 1 (theta > 47.06 deg from the axis)
  Geometric meaning: the escape cone on the lamp's sky is
     { n_phi > 0, n_theta^2 < (1/k^2 - 1) n_phi^2, and (n_phi > k or n_r > 0) }
  (n = unit direction in the ZAMO frame: radial, polar, azimuthal components).
  So photons escape through a wedge around the rotation direction; within the wedge, those
  with n_phi > k escape even if aimed inward (they bounce off the potential barrier).

Decimal values:
  a=M, equator: 0.29166666...   a=0.9M: 0.26736...   a=0.7M: 0.19480...   a=0.55M: 0.09110...
  a=M, theta=70 deg: 0.25013...  a=0.8M, theta=60 deg: 0.13282...  a=0.9M, theta=50 deg: 0.04842...

How it was derived:
  Near-horizon expansion r = M(1 + eps x), photon angular momentum
  lambda = lambda_H - eps u M/a with lambda_H = (M^2 + a^2)/a. In the ZAMO frame the photon
  direction is n_phi = Sigma_H/(a sin(theta) v), n_theta = sqrt(Theta)/v, with v = u + 2,
  Sigma_H = M^2 + a^2 cos^2 theta; the radial potential becomes, in the far zone,
  R = (r-M)^2 [(r+M)^2 - C] and, in the near zone, eps^2 [(2x+u)^2 - C x^2], with
  C = eta + M^2/a^2 = v^2 (n_theta^2 + n_phi^2). Escape requires C < 4 (no outer turning
  point) and either u < 0 (any direction: inward photons bounce at x = |u|/(2 - sqrt C) <= 1)
  or u > 0 with outward motion. Translating to directions gives the wedge above, whose solid
  angle is 2(pi/2 - arcsin k) + 2 Int_{arcsin k}^{pi/2} sqrt(sin^2 - k^2)/sin
  = pi - 2 arcsin k + pi (1 - k)  (the last integral equals (pi/2)(1-k), checked to 30 digits).
  For a = M this agrees with the near-horizon "Case II" edge of Baines & Visser
  (arXiv:2405.08875), sin(Theta) = sin(Phi_edge)/sin(Phi).

How it was verified:
  * Independent brute-force ray test (code/escape_kn2.py): isotropic grid of directions in the
    ZAMO frame at r = M(1 + delta), conserved quantities computed with cancellation-free
    algebra, escape decided by scanning the exact radial potential (including photons sent
    inward that bounce back).  Results (grid N = 300, delta = 1e-7 and 1e-9 identical):
        a=1,   90 deg: sim 0.29172   formula 0.29167
        a=0.7, 90 deg: sim 0.19473   formula 0.19480
        a=0.8, 60 deg: sim 0.13237 / 0.13409 / 0.13321 (N=300/150/600)  formula 0.13282
        a=0.9, 50 deg: sim 0.04807 / 0.04809 / 0.04880 (N=300/150/600)  formula 0.04842
    (grid noise ~1e-3 because the escape region has sharp edges).
  * Literature numbers: Ogasawara, Igata, Harada & Miyamoto, PRD 101, 044023 (2020),
    arXiv:1910.01528, Table II (horizon limit, equator, extremal Kerr-Newman), numerically:
        a = 1: 0.291, 0.9: 0.267, 0.7: 0.194/0.195, 0.55: 0.0911, 0.5: 0
    Formula: 0.29167, 0.26736, 0.19480, 0.09110, 0 -- all match.
  * Threshold a > M/2 for nonzero escape matches that paper's main result.

Novelty status: NOT FOUND (general formula). The equatorial uncharged value 7/24 is KNOWN
  (Igata et al., e.g. arXiv:2108.09051 / EPJC 2021, "Photon emissions from near-horizon
  extremal and near-extremal Kerr equatorial emitters"). The charged equatorial values were
  previously only known numerically (arXiv:1910.01528); the latitude dependence was not found.
  Caveat: "Escape probability for isotropic emitters near Kerr black hole with astrometric
  observables", Commun. Theor. Phys. (2024) 055404, describes a general (apparently numerical)
  method for emitters anywhere, including off the equator; we could not read its full text, so
  it may contain numerical values overlapping the Kerr (a = M) off-equator case. Ogasawara &
  Igata (arXiv:2111.03243) classify off-equator escape regions and announced a follow-up on
  probabilities, which we did not find.

[8] ESCAPE PROBABILITY FROM THE INNERMOST STABLE ORBIT OF AN EXTREMAL KERR-NEWMAN BLACK HOLE
==================================================================================
Question (plain English):
  Gas in an accretion disk spirals in until the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO). For a
  maximally spinning black hole that orbit hugs the horizon, yet a lamp riding on it still
  gets most of its light out: for the uncharged case the known exact answer is
  5/12 + arctan(sqrt(5/3))/(sqrt5 pi) = 54.65%.  What happens for a charged (Kerr-Newman)
  black hole with the maximal combination a^2 + Q^2 = M^2?

Results (M = 1, extremal Kerr-Newman, equatorial plane):
  (i)  Near the horizon, every circular orbit moves relative to the local non-rotating observer
       (ZAMO) at speed  v -> 1/(2a)  (= 1/2 for Kerr), i.e. v = k with k from entry [6].
  (ii) The ISCO sits on the horizon exactly when a >= 1/sqrt(2); at the horizon
       dE/dr ~ 4 (a - 1/sqrt2) (E = orbital energy).
  (iii) For a >= 1/sqrt(2), with k = 1/(2a) in [1/2, 1/sqrt2], a lamp on the ISCO, isotropic in
       its own frame, has escape probability
         P_ISCO = 1/2 - arcsin(k)/(2 pi) + k * arctan( sqrt((1+k^2)/(1-k^2)) ) / (pi sqrt(1+k^2))
       (arctan(sqrt((1+k^2)/(1-k^2))) = arcsin(sqrt((1+k^2)/2))).
       * a = 1   (k = 1/2):       5/12 + arctan(sqrt(5/3))/(sqrt5 pi) = 0.546454899648778864915755...  (known)
       * a = 0.8 (k = 0.625):     0.558898...
       * a = 1/sqrt2 (k = 1/sqrt2): 3/8 + sqrt(3)/9 = 3/8 + 1/(3 sqrt 3) = 0.567450089...  (new, neat)
       So adding charge (lowering spin) slightly INCREASES the ISCO escape probability.
  (iv) Corollary: the maximum blueshift E_inf/E_emit of escaping photons from the ISCO lamp is
       a sqrt((2a+1)/(2a-1))  (photon emitted straight along the motion, t = 1):
       sqrt3 for Kerr (known), 1 + 1/sqrt2 = 1.7071... at a = 1/sqrt2, 1.66533 at a = 0.8.
       Ray-simulation maxima (grid N=200, cannot hit t=1 exactly): 1.72628, 1.70211, 1.66021.

How it was derived:
  Take the near-horizon escape cone of entry [6] (in the ZAMO frame), boost the lamp along the
  rotation direction with speed v, and integrate over the lamp's own isotropic directions
  (code/boosted.py). With v = k the region simplifies: in the lamp's frame every direction
  with n'_phi = t > 0 escapes, none with t < -k escape, and for -k < t < 0 the escaping
  fraction of the circle of directions is (1/pi) arcsin((t+k)/(k sqrt(1-t^2))), giving
     P = 1/2 + (1/2pi) Int_{-k}^{0} arcsin( (t+k)/(k sqrt(1-t^2)) ) dt,
  and integration by parts + partial fractions gives the closed form above. Full proof:
  with b = 1 + k^2, by parts J = Int_0^k s(1-ks)/((1-s^2) sqrt(s(2k-bs))) ds, and
  s(1-ks)/(1-s^2) = k + (1-k)/(2(1-s)) - (1+k)/(2(1+s)). Substituting s = (2k/b) sin^2(phi)
  (upper limit sin^2(phi_1) = b/2, so tan(phi_1)/sqrt(b) = 1/sqrt(1-k^2)):
     k * Int ds/sqrt(..)            = (2k/sqrt b) arcsin(sqrt(b/2)),
     (1-k)/2 * Int ds/((1-s)sqrt..) = arctan( sqrt((1-k)/(1+k)) ),
     (1+k)/2 * Int ds/((1+s)sqrt..) = arctan( sqrt((1+k)/(1-k)) ),
  and arctan(x) - arctan(1/x) = 2 arctan(x) - pi/2 = arccos(k) - pi/2 = -arcsin(k) for
  x = sqrt((1-k)/(1+k)). Hence J = (2k/sqrt(1+k^2)) arcsin(sqrt((1+k^2)/2)) - arcsin(k).
  (Also confirmed numerically to 30 digits at k = 1/2, 5/9, 2/3, 0.9.)
  The orbital speed v and the ISCO threshold came from direct numerics on the Kerr-Newman
  circular-orbit formulas (code/kn_isco.py): v(r -> 1) = 0.83333333 (a=0.6), 0.71428571 (a=0.7),
  0.55555556 (a=0.9), 0.5 (a=1); ISCO bisection gives a_c = 0.70710678 (= 1/sqrt2), and
  dE/dr at r = 1 + 1e-20 is -4.0e-9 / +4.0e-9 for a = 1/sqrt2 -/+ 1e-9.

How it was verified:
  * The boosted-cone calculation reproduces BOTH known Kerr numbers to 25 digits:
    v = 0 -> 7/24 = 0.29166666...; v = 1/2 -> 0.5464548996487788649157551 (known ISCO value).
  * Independent brute-force ray test (code/escape_isco_sim.py): grid of 180,000 directions
    isotropic in the moving lamp's frame, Lorentz-transformed to the ZAMO frame at
    r = 1 + 1e-7, escape decided by the exact radial potential:
        a = 1:        sim 0.54644   formula 0.54645
        a = 0.8:      sim 0.55890   formula 0.55890
        a = 1/sqrt2:  sim 0.56749   formula 0.56745
  * Integral representation vs closed form: identical to 30 digits for k = 1/2, 5/9, 2/3.

Novelty status: NOT FOUND for the Kerr-Newman formula and the 3/8 + sqrt3/9 value.
  KNOWN for the Kerr value (a = 1): Igata et al. (Ogasawara-Igata, e.g. arXiv:1910.12682,

                   - ( 31/2048 - 3C/1024 - 9C^2/2048 ) a^4
                   - ( 233 - 69C - 105C^2 + 5C^3 ) a^6 / 32768 - ... ]
   * Surprise: at order a^2 the correction (-a^2/16) is the SAME for every direction of approach.
     (Contrast: for light, entry [5], the a^2 term is (1 + cos^2 theta)/18, strongly direction-dependent.)
   * Averaged over isotropic directions (<C^n> = 1/(2n+1)):
        <sigma> = 16 pi (M/v)^2 [ 1 - a^2/16 - 17 a^4/1280 - 83 a^6/14336 - ... ]
   * Exact general-direction value as one integral (code/mb_incl.py):
        A(a, theta) = (2/sin^2 theta) Int sqrt(N(u)) |3u^4 + 4u^3 + 1 - a^2| / (a^2 u^3) du,
        N(u) = sin^2 theta (1+u)^4 (a^2 - (u^2-1)^2) - cos^2 theta ((1+u)^4 - 2(1+u)^3 + a^2)^2,
        between the two largest positive roots of N  (sigma = A (M/v)^2). N has degree 8 (genus 3),
        so only the edge-on case [15] has a simple closed form.
   Example values (a = 0.5): edge-on 49.4259440025740610119469; 60 deg: 49.4298087887702480091;
     1 deg from the axis: 49.4538578142693457866.

How it was derived: 12-point fits in a^2 (a^2 = 1e-4 ... 1.2e-3, 60-digit arithmetic) at six values
  of C (1/16, 3/16, ..., 11/16), then exact polynomial fits in C; residuals 5e-44 (a^2), 1e-39 (a^4),
  5e-36 (a^6); rational recognition errors < 2e-34.

How it was verified:
  * C = 0 reproduces the exact edge-on series of [15] (1/16, 31/2048, 233/32768).
  * C = 1 (approach along the spin axis) checked independently with sympy from the exact polar
    case: the capture region is a disc of radius^2 = Q(y_0), y_0^4 - 2 y_0^3 + a^2 = 0, giving
    1 - a^2/16 - a^4/128 - a^6/512, identical to the fitted polynomials at C = 1.
  * The general-direction integral reproduces [15] at 90 deg to 50 digits.

Novelty status: NOT FOUND. arXiv:2510.13604 fits spin expansions numerically (~1.5%); we did not
  find the exact rational coefficients or the direction-independence of the a^2 term stated.

==================================================================================
[17] SPIN CORRECTION TO THE CAPTURE CROSS-SECTION OF A BLACK HOLE AT ANY SPEED AND DIRECTION
==================================================================================
Question (plain English):
  A black hole moving through a gas of particles (anything from slow dark matter to light)
  captures those within a certain "target area". Without spin this area is known exactly for any
  speed. How does a small spin change it, for any speed and any direction of approach?
  Entries [5] (light) and [16] (slow particles) are the two ends of this question.

Formula (G = c = M = 1; particles arriving with speed v at angle theta to the spin axis; C = cos^2 theta):
   sigma(v, a, theta) = sigma_S(v) * [ 1 - a^2 * ( r_c + 3 C (4 - r_c) ) / ( 2 r_c^2 (6 - r_c) ) + O(a^4) ]
   with r_c(v) = ( 4v^2 - 1 + sqrt(1 + 8 v^2) ) / (2 v^2)   (radius of the Schwarzschild unstable
   circular orbit with the particles' energy; r_c = 4 for v -> 0, r_c = 3 for v = 1), and the
   known non-spinning value sigma_S(v) = pi r_c^2 / ( (r_c - 3)(E^2 - 1) ), E = 1/sqrt(1 - v^2).
   * Side-on (C = 0):        correction a^2 / ( 2 r_c (6 - r_c) )
   * Along the axis (C = 1): correction a^2 / r_c^2
   * Averaged over directions (<C> = 1/3):  correction 2 a^2 / ( r_c^2 (6 - r_c) )
   Checks against the ends: light (r_c = 3): (1 + C)/18 = entry [5]; slow (r_c = 4): 1/16 for every
   direction = entry [16]. For light the direction average 2/27 reproduces <A> = 27 pi - 2 pi a^2.

How it was derived and verified:
  * General-energy spherical orbits L(r), Q(r) from sympy (R = R' = 0 with energy E;
    code/anyv.py), capture area A = (2/(p^2 sin theta)) Int sqrt(Q + a^2 p^2 cos^2 - L^2 cot^2) |dL|
    (p^2 = E^2 - 1) evaluated at 40 digits (code/anyv_area.py) for a = 0.004 ... 0.016 and fitted
    in a^2 (code/anyv_f2.py).
  * Speeds chosen so that r_c is rational (r_c = 15/4, 7/2, 10/3, 13/4, 31/10 via
    E^2 = (r-2)^2/(r(r-3))). Side-on coefficients came out 8/135, 2/35, 9/160, 8/143, i.e. exactly
    1/(2 r (6 - r)). Along the axis (exact disc formula, code/anyv_pole.py): 16/225, 4/49, 9/100,
    16/169, 100/961 = 1/r^2 (agreement 1e-18).
  * Linearity in cos^2 theta PREDICTED and then confirmed at three new (r_c, C) pairs:
      (7/2, 1/4): 0.0632653061224614 vs predicted 0.0632653061224490
      (13/4, 3/4): 0.0849919311457859 vs 0.0849919311457773
      (15/4, 1/2): 0.0651851851851951 vs 0.0651851851851852
  Status: identified numerically to ~13 digits at 11 test points and consistent with both exact
  limits; not proven analytically.

Novelty status: NOT FOUND. arXiv:2510.13604 (2025) expands the Kerr accretion cross-section in spin
  with numerically FITTED coefficient functions (~1.5% accuracy); this gives the exact a^2 coefficient.

ADDENDUM to [6], [7], [8]: braneworld ("tidal charge") black holes with a > M
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The Kerr-Newman geodesic equations depend on the charge only through Q^2. Braneworld black
  holes (Dadhich et al.) have the same metric with Q^2 -> -beta < 0, so "extremal" means
  a^2 - beta = M^2 and the spin can EXCEED M. All derivations above used only a^2 + Q^2 = M^2,
  so the formulas continue to a > M. Checked numerically:
    [7] area, a = 1.1 / 1.3 / 1.6:  formula 81.509244123 / 93.044259764 / 112.640571550
                                    polygon 81.509244118 / 93.044259750 / 112.640571535
    [6] escape, a = 1.3, equator:   formula 0.34101,  ray simulation 0.34138 (grid N=200)
    [8] ISCO escape, a = 1.3:       formula 0.53539,  ray simulation 0.53535 (grid N=200);
        the ISCO stays on the horizon (no energy minimum above it, code/kn_isco.py).
  Trend: as a -> infinity (k -> 0) the ZAMO escape probability -> 1/2 and the ISCO escape
  probability -> 1/2 as well; the ISCO value decreases monotonically from 3/8 + sqrt3/9
  (a = M/sqrt2) through 54.65% (a = M) to 1/2.
